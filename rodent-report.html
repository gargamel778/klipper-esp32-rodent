Firmware optimisations that survived

Klipper was ported onto a BTT Rodent V1.1 — an ESP32-based CNC board with four TMC2160 drivers whose step, direction and enable lines all hang off a 74HC595 shift register. It works: all four axes drive real motors, every commanded step pulse arrives at every driver, and the board completes a SpeedBoatRace-legal 3DBenchy in 3 minutes 45 seconds, ten times out of ten, at 800 mm/s with 100k acceleration and a square-corner velocity of 30.

The result that matters

At 0.25 mm layers and 0.5 mm line width the profile extrudes 0.125 mm² per mm of path, so 800 mm/s is exactly 100 mm³/s — the melt-rate ceiling of a high-end hotend. The board’s reliable maximum lands precisely where the hotend gives out, at motion settings a purpose-built machine could genuinely run.

The controller is not the bottleneck. That is a more useful claim than any speed record, and unlike the headline this report originally carried, it survives repetition.

The engineering value, though, is mostly negative results. Three separate measurements pointed confidently in the wrong direction, and each was only caught because a later number refused to cohere with it:

  • A synthetic CoreXY workload reported a hard ceiling at 300 mm/s and attributed it to firmware scheduling. It was the serial baud rate. Raising it doubled the envelope.
  • A shift-register optimisation benchmarked +54% and passed a driver-level pulse-integrity test 40/40. Under real four-stepper load it shut the MCU down in 27 moves, where stock firmware ran 1716 clean.
  • The synthetic protocol was assumed to under-represent a real print. It was far harsher — commanding 32,000–80,000 steps/s per motor where a real Benchy is accel-limited to ~6,000.
  • A “1000 mm/s ceiling” was published from a single run. Repeated properly it completes 3 times in 8 — the published figure would have lost two jobs in three.
The single most transferable finding

In Klipper's planner, junction_deviation = SCV²·(√2−1)/max_accel and move_jd_v2 = R_jd · junction_deviation · accel. The acceleration cancels. On geometry with a sub-millimetre median segment — which is every real print — square_corner_velocity sets the speed and acceleration cannot touch it. Commanding 5× the feedrate and 8× the acceleration moved one measured ramp by 19%.

Standing caveat on every number here

These are bench measurements with unloaded steppers, no hotend and no filament. Every result means the electronics kept up — never the machine moved. At 900 mm/s the pulleys spin but stall under finger pressure. Timer too close is an MCU scheduling failure and is independent of whether the rotor follows, so the electronic ceiling transfers to a capable machine; the print times do not.

01Origin & motivation

The project began as a documentation question: could an ESP32 serve as a secondary MCU under Klipper, handling GPIO only, with motion left to a real board? Klipper's architecture makes that plausible — the host does all planning and step generation, and the MCU is a timed I/O engine behind a well-defined HAL in src/generic/.

The scope then widened deliberately: ignore Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, ignore the "no motion system" caveat, and port the whole thing — including as a primary MCU. Two community forks existed; fermino/klipper-esp32-port was chosen for its more complete HAL and its existing 74HC595 support, which matters because the target board routes all step/dir/enable through one.

The target hardware was chosen for availability rather than suitability: a BTT Rodent V1.1, of which two spares existed. It is a classic Xtensa ESP32, not an S3 — which turns out to be load-bearing, because this port derives Klipper's timer from the Xtensa CCOUNT/CCOMPARE core registers, which the RISC-V ESP32 variants (C3/C6/H2) do not have; timer.c hard-#errors on a non-Xtensa target. That is a constraint of this port rather than of the silicon: the RISC-V parts expose a SYSTIMER peripheral whose TARGET0 comparator provides an equivalent compare interrupt, and a separate ESP32 port (kluoyun/klipper, branch dev/esp32, published 2026-08-31) drives an ESP32-C3 that way. The trade is resolution — SYSTIMER runs at 16 MHz against CCOUNT's 240 MHz, about 15× coarser per tick.

Explicit non-goals, set at the outset: no collaboration with the upstream fork author, no reduction below 16 microsteps (the established 3D-printing minimum), and no changes to pulley sizes. Optimisation had to come from the firmware and the configuration, not from making the mechanics easier.

The drivers are TMC2160, not TMC5160. Earlier notes in this project said 5160 because the board's FluidNC configuration declares them that way — FluidNC has no direct TMC2160 support, so the 5160 driver is used as a stand-in.

The substitution is legitimate in both firmwares for the same reason: the TMC2160 is a TMC5160 without the integrated motion controller. The ramp generator is absent, but the driver half — GCONF, CHOPCONF, IHOLD_IRUN, GLOBALSCALER, COOLCONF, PWMCONF, DRV_STATUS, MSCNT, the microstep tables — is shared. Klipper drives these purely in step/dir mode and touches only those registers, so a [tmc5160] section is correct and complete for this hardware. Every register-level result in this report, including the MSCNT verification, holds unchanged.

Board-level findings

Sense resistor — a 3.4× current error waiting to happen

The V1.1 board carries R075 (0.075 Ω) sense resistors. BTT's published configuration specifies 0.022. Using the published value would command roughly 3.4× the intended motor current. Verified visually on the board; the remaining resistors sit under the heatsink. Independently confirmed by the official manual, whose V1.1 pin diagram labels the part RSENSE 75MR.

  • USB-C has no CC pull-downs. A C-to-C cable will never enumerate; a legacy A-to-C cable is required.
  • macOS 26 will not bind the CH340K. Apple's built-in AppleUSBCHCOM does not claim PID 0x7522, and a duplicate third-party dext conflicts with it. CLI-active is not the same as UI-active on this OS version.
  • Brownout under USB power. Flash reads of 3 KB succeeded; 192 KB and 4 MB reads dropped the port entirely. Connecting the bench supply resolved it. Any full-flash backup needs external power.

Pin map verification

The shift-register bit ordering and the TMC daisy-chain position were both verified electrically rather than assumed: SRn maps to I2SO.n, and spi_index N corresponds to chain_position N. Each of the four ports was confirmed by driving one axis at a time and observing both rotation and the corresponding driver's DRV_STATUS open-load flags.

The manual's own pin table matches the empirically derived map exactly — STEP I2SO.2/5/10/13, DIR I2SO.1/4/9/12, EN I2SO.0/7/8/15, CS gpio.5, SCK gpio.18, SDI gpio.19, SDO gpio.23. Deriving it from the hardware first was still worthwhile: it is what made the R075 discrepancy visible rather than inherited from a config file.

04First motion, and how it was verified

Motors were added one at a time, drivers disabled between insertions, with each axis confirmed to rotate in both directions before the next was connected. Current was set conservatively at 0.8 A into 2.5 A motors.

Rotation alone is weak evidence, so pulse integrity was measured at the driver. The TMC2160 exposes MSCNT (register 0x6A), a 10-bit microstep-table counter that advances on every STEP pulse the driver receives. It sits on the far side of the shift register and is independent of anything the MCU believes. At microsteps: 16, each pulse advances it by exactly 256/16 = 16.

expected MSCNT delta = (N × 16) mod 1024, signed by direction

Result: 40 of 40 cases exact — all four axes, both directions, from 8 pulses up to 12,037 pulses at 64,000 steps/s.

Two traps in this test

MSCNT counts down for a positive move on this DIR polarity, so the test must calibrate direction per axis rather than assume it. And any test distance whose residue is ±512 is useless — 512 is its own negation mod 1024, so it cannot distinguish a correct move from a perfectly reversed one. The first run of this test reported 24/32 "failures" that were entirely the first mistake.

Rates are individually good or bad — this is not a speed limit

1500000 outperforms 2000000 decisively, which rules out bandwidth as the mechanism and points at divisor/timing error per rate on the CH340. There are two distinct failure modes, and the second is dangerous:

460800 and 250000 fail at identify — you never connect, and the problem is obvious. 2000000 reports ready, drives every axis correctly in both directions, and passes the axis test. It then collapses at 60 mm/s under real motion. A rate can be bad in a way that no quick check will reveal.

A refinement worth recording: at 1.5 Mbps the link carries ~150,000 bytes/s and the workload used 4–8 kB/s — about 3–5% utilisation. The baud effect is therefore latency, not bandwidth. A 64-byte message takes 2.6 ms at 250000 and 0.43 ms at 1500000, and Timer too close is by definition a late-arrival failure.

The synthetic protocol was far harsher than any real print

It issued 9–15 moves/s with 43–69 mm segments at 400–1000 mm/s: 32,000–80,000 steps/s per motor. Real sliced geometry has a 0.5 mm median segment and is accel-limited to roughly 6,000 steps/s. Every envelope figure derived from it is pessimistic. The firmware comparisons remain valid because they were internally controlled; the absolute speeds do not transfer.

The protocol also had a genuine defect found late: its 45° infill always used the same diagonal, so in CoreXY one motor carried all infill for the whole run. Load balance measured 0.59. Alternating the diagonal per layer — and alternating the two objects between main- and anti-diagonal layouts, because travel between them was 0.205 balanced — brought it to 0.94–0.995. A dead statement in the infill loop had also disabled zigzag alternation entirely.

07Firmware optimisation: the shift register

Every step pulse on this board passes through a 74HC595. The first useful measurement was to quantify what that costs, by running Klipper's own three-stepper benchmark on SR pins and then on direct GPIO — same board, same firmware, same baud.

Two independently harmful changes, two different mechanisms

1 · The CCOUNT deadline is unsafe

Skipping the hardware handshake because elapsed time "proves" the transfer finished fails under four concurrent steppers, where steps land sub-microsecond apart. 27 moves before shutdown. Single-axis tests cannot see it: even at 64,000 steps/s, steps on one axis are ~15 µs apart, so the deadline has always expired and the fast path is never exercised.

2 · The plain store removes an implicit bus barrier

spi_ll_user_start() is hw->cmd.usr = 1 — a bitfield write to a volatile register, which the compiler must implement as an APB read, an OR, and an APB write. That read forces the preceding posted data_buf write to land before the transfer starts. Replacing it with a bare store removes the barrier, and the SPI can begin shifting before the FIFO data has arrived.

The only safe change is v7: hoisting esp_rom_get_cpu_ticks_per_us() — a ROM call, neither const nor pure, so the compiler cannot lift it — out of the wait loop. It shows zero benchmark gain, because the call only executes when the loop actually spins, yet is consistently better under real load.

Also fixed in passing

spi_ll_write_buffer() memcpy's 4 bytes per data_buf slot out of a CONFIG_SR_BYTE_NO-sized buffer — reading two bytes past the end of a stack buffer on every step when that value is 2.

What actually loads this board

Not move count — sustained velocity. Three changes to the file mattered more than every motion parameter combined:

  1. Remove z-hops. Z is a TR8x8 leadscrew at 8× the steps/mm of XY, capped at max_z_velocity 100 / max_z_accel 2000. Every hop is a slow, step-dense interruption that fragments fast motion into bursts the board absorbs trivially.
  2. Remove retraction. Each retract/unretract changes the extrusion ratio, so instantaneous_corner_velocity caps that junction hard.
  3. Slice fast. With nothing interrupting, the toolhead finally sustains velocity — and sustained velocity is the only thing this board has ever been sensitive to.

10The motion maths

Read straight from klippy/toolhead.py rather than from documentation:

junction_deviation = SCV² × (√2 − 1) / max_accel
move_jd_v2 = R_jd × junction_deviation × accel
R_jd = sin(θ/2) / (1 − sin(θ/2)), where sin(θ/2) = cos(φ/2)

⇒ v_junction = SCV × √(R_jd × 0.41421) — acceleration cancels

At φ = 90° this reduces to exactly SCV, which is the parameter's definition. The competing limits keep their acceleration dependence:

v_centripetal = √(0.5 × d × accel / tan(φ/2))
v_extruder = ICV / |Δ(E per mm)|

Two API facts worth knowing

SET_VELOCITY_LIMIT does not clamp to the configured max_accel / max_velocity — it assigns directly, so runtime values can far exceed the config. And ACCEL_TO_DECEL does not exist in this Klipper version; only VELOCITY, ACCEL, SQUARE_CORNER_VELOCITY and MINIMUM_CRUISE_RATIO are read. Passing it is silently ignored.

11The speed Benchy, and finding a speed that actually holds

The SpeedBoatRace rules constrain the profile rather than the motion, which is what makes the comparison meaningful: ≤0.25 mm layers, 2 walls, 10% infill, 3 top/bottom layers, ≤0.5 mm nozzle or line width, PLA/PETG/ASA/ABS, dimensionally accurate, stepper-driven.

Mean feedrate jumping 265 → 500 mm/s is the tell: the previous file’s average was dragged down by slow retract and unretract moves. Z moves falling 1,980 → 192 is one per layer — every hop gone.

The first ceiling was published from a single run, and was wrong

An initial search found 1000 mm/s completing and 1050 failing, and this report originally stated that as the ceiling. Repeating it destroyed the claim: 1000 mm/s completes 3 times in 8, and 950 completes 7 times in 13. Both finish in the same 3:30 when they finish, so the faster setting bought nothing and lost the job roughly two-thirds of the time.

The same standard had already been applied rigorously to the firmware comparison — six baseline runs, counterbalanced ordering, paired statistics — and was then abandoned the moment the question changed from which firmware to how fast.

The failure is stochastic, not a threshold

Across 16 full prints the failures landed at 4.7, 9.5, 10.0, 10.0, 10.8, 12.2, 20.9, 23.4, 25.9, 26.9, 86.0, 87.2 and 89.3% of the file — three tight clusters rather than the uniform spread a constant hazard would give. Binning the file into 2% slices and measuring what each demands:

Geometry where failures cluster vs everywhere else
Bucketsmean junction anglemean segment
where failures occurred (n=7)24.7°1.92 mm
all others (n=43)29.8°1.41 mm

Failures cluster where segments are 36% longer and junctions shallower — both of which let the toolhead reach and hold higher velocity. That is the same mechanism as everything else in this report: sustained velocity is what loads this board, so it fails in the specific stretches of geometry that permit it.

Thermal was tested and ruled out

Success rate appeared to degrade across a session (first 6 runs 5/6, last 10 runs 3/10), suggesting heat. A cooldown test — motors de-energised, 25 minutes idle — returned 2/5, identical to the hot batch’s 2/5. TMC over-temperature flags were clear (otpw=0, ot=0) on all four drivers immediately after an hour of load, the host was idle (load 0.39, a 12 MB log read in 51 ms), and driver heatsinks measured ~40 °C against a ~120 °C warning threshold. The apparent degradation was clustering in a small sample.

Staircase search: 10 runs per level

Ten runs at a speed; any failure drops 100 mm/s, a clean 10/10 steps up 50, and the search terminates when an established level has a known failure 50 above it. Currents were raised to 1.4 A on X/Y/Z and 0.6 A on E for these runs (verified at the drivers via GLOBALSCALER/IRUN).

850 mm/s failed on run 10 of 10. At a five-run sample it would have been declared established and been wrong — the clearest possible argument for the sample size.

Why 800 mm/s is the answer, and not an arbitrary one

At 0.25 × 0.5 mm the profile extrudes 0.125 mm² per mm, so 800 mm/s = 100 mm³/s. That is the melt rate of a high-end hotend. Above it the print is flow-limited no matter how good the motion system is — the 1000 mm/s slice demands 125 mm³/s, which no such hotend can deliver, so the slicer’s 3m37s estimate describes motion that could never be extruded.

SCV 50 is not a real-machine setting either (tuned printers run 5–15; aggressive builds 20–30), which makes the 750 mm/s / SCV 50 result academic. 800 mm/s at SCV 30 and 100k acceleration is a configuration a purpose-built machine could actually run, and the board holds it 10/10.

Higher motor current changed nothing

Raising X/Y/Z from 0.8 to 1.4 A had no measurable effect on reliability, as predicted before the runs: Timer too close is an MCU scheduling failure and is independent of motor torque. At 0.8 A, 950 mm/s gave 7/13; at 1.4 A, 900 gave 3/4 and 800 gave 5/6 — the same marginal behaviour. What the current did buy is mechanical: motor cases at 65–75 °C, driver heatsinks at ~40 °C, and a supply draw of 1.07–1.15 A at 36 V under load against 0.73 A holding.

PSU current is not motor current

The TMC2160 is a switching regulator: it PWMs the 36 V rail to force 1.4 A through a coil needing only a few volts. Naive intuition says “4 motors × 1.4 A = 5.6 A”; the measured draw is ~1.1 A, about 5× lower. Working backwards from the 0.73 A holding figure gives a winding resistance of ~1.9 Ω per phase, which is normal for this motor class — the power model is consistent.

12Measurement traps

Six errors were caught during this work. Every one of them was found because a later number refused to cohere with an earlier one — not because anything was checked carefully in advance. They are the most portable output of the project.

01 · Klipper's step-rate benchmark predicts nothing, in either direction

It drives three steppers at a fixed interval with nothing else running — no four-stepper contention, no coordinated moves, no pressure advance, no serial traffic. It endorsed every build that later shut down, and scored the one genuinely good change at exactly zero.

02 · A correctness test that cannot reach the failure mode is not evidence

MSCNT pulse integrity passed 40/40 on firmware that died in 27 moves. It drives one axis at a time, so steps are ~15 µs apart; it structurally cannot exercise the sub-microsecond inter-stepper spacing where those changes break.

03 · On MCU shutdown, Klipper leaves print_stats at paused, not error

A completion loop polling print_stats alone reports a dead printer as healthy indefinitely — it did so here for two minutes, printing stalls 0 the whole time. Check klippy's own info state.

04 · Single runs are not evidence — and knowing that is not the same as applying it

Baseline is repeatable to ±0.5% retransmits and lands on exactly 1716 moves every time — but that took six runs to establish. The failing builds died at 27 / 87 / 159 / 623 moves, so a single run could have been read either way. Ceiling as a metric turned out to have a 300–400 mm/s spread on identical firmware.

The discipline was then dropped for the speed question in the same session: a 1000 mm/s ceiling was published from n=1 and is really 3/8. Later, 850 mm/s passed nine consecutive runs and failed the tenth — a five-run protocol would have certified it.

05 · A bisection inherited its bound from a different file

The upper bound came from a run on a file whose travel_speed = 100000 emitted F6000000 travels. The bisection was about to report the ceiling as 975 mm/s — 20% low — and blame the board for a slicer typo. Three identical 3:30 runs cannot bracket a failure 25 mm/s away; that inconsistency is what exposed it.

06 · pgrep -f foo.sh in a wait loop matches its own command line

The shell running the loop contains the pattern, so the loop sees itself and never exits. Cost about twenty minutes of wall-clock across two occurrences, once as pkill that killed its own SSH session. Use a bracket pattern: pgrep -f "fo[o].sh".

13Open questions

  • What sets the failure, mechanistically, is still unknown. It is Timer too close — an MCU timer deadline missed — and it correlates with sustained velocity and with specific high-velocity geometry. But thermal is ruled out, the host is idle, the link runs at 3–5% utilisation, and motor current is irrelevant. The proximate cause inside the firmware has not been isolated.
  • The 40% shift-register headroom is real and unclaimed. It is CPU-side APB traffic, not shift duration. Any future attempt must keep both the hardware handshake and the read-modify-write barrier, and be gated on a four-stepper load test rather than a benchmark.
  • CAN is feasible to write but blocked on hardware here — see the investigation note in the working report. ~100–150 lines against Klipper's three-function arch contract, but the board has no CAN transceiver, and bridge mode is impossible on the classic ESP32 (no native USB device).
  • Hardware SPI on SPI3 is an empty stub. It would speed the MCU's side of a TMC transfer but not reduce the serial traffic those transfers generate — likely a smaller win than it appears. The cheaper experiment is reducing TMC polling frequency, which is config-only.
  • The 125 s floor on the quality Benchy was never fully explained. A look-ahead simulator built from Klipper's own source reproduces the control to −2% but over-predicts by 57% at the top of the ramp.
  • Klipper has no Modbus support, so the Huanyang VFD this board previously drove under FluidNC has no equivalent — a real regression for CNC use. Its UART1 (GPIO15/16 TX/RX, GPIO14 RTS) sits unused.
  • Step loss is not directly measurable — there are no encoders. MSCNT proves pulses reach the driver, not that the rotor followed. At 800 mm/s the motors certainly do not follow.

Test protocol that finally discriminated

A SpeedBoatRace-legal Benchy with z-hops and retraction disabled, streamed through [virtual_sdcard], run ten times at a fixed setting. It is the only workload found that meaningfully loads this board, and it is the gate any future firmware change should be measured against. Ten runs, not three: 850 mm/s passed nine and failed the tenth.
